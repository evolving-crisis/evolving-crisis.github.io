### 4.2. Comparing species tree topologies across methods



From the "treespace" plot (Fig. 8), the species tree topologies tended to cluster into groups first based on whether their alignments were paralog-filtered, either through the additional trimming done by the PPD pipeline or by the Yang & Smith pipeline, although this effect was only seen for HybPiper-based analyses and not the CAPTUS-based analyses. The topological instability in these HybPiper-based trees, along with the lower paralog recovery in HybPiper (Figs. 3 & 4), could be caused by the stochastic nature of paralog recovery in HybPiper, insufficient sequencing depth, or the assembly of "chimeric sequences". The next strongest determinant of species tree topologies was whether the trees were constructed using the ASTRAL coalescent-based or a concatenation-based method.

Across all tanglegram comparisons (Supplementary Figs. 9a–k), tree topologies were similar, except for a few occasions where differences occurred on some backbone nodes or within a few genera which indicate areas of true biological incongruence rather than a lack of data. Genera such as *Sindora* and *Daniellia*, and the Schotieae and Barnebydendreae tribes often have branches with low Quartet Concordance (QC) scores (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 7a–i), which shows high incongruence among gene trees in these genera and tribes, regardless of dataset. QD values close to 0 show a skewed distribution of alternative quartets, suggesting introgression or hybridisation, and QD values close to 1 show more equally distributed discordant quartets, indicative of the random process of incomplete lineage sorting (ILS). Across all datasets, regardless of paralog handling, QD values in *Sindora* and *Daniellia* often ranged between 0 and 1, which suggests both ILS and the possibility of reticulate evolution. In addition, the relationships of the tribes Schotieae and Barnebydendreae are known to be unstable and can change depending on whether plastome or nuclear DNA is used, which may reflect the presence of ancient hybridisation.

The Quartet Informativeness (QI) values were the lowest in the two datasets with the PPD trimmed alignments (`HybPiper PPDdegentrimmed`, `HybPiper PPDdegentrimmedrem77`) (Supplementary Figs. 7c, d), which shows that the trimming of paralogous regions led to a decrease in informative characters for species tree construction. Nevertheless, the species tree topologies were generally consistent, with any differences restricted to the same genera and deeper nodes as in the other tree comparisons (Supplementary Figs. 9b, c).

The presence of ILS and potential hybridisation is further suggested by the DensiTree plots (Fig. 7, Supplementary Fig. 8a–i) where there is a lack of a clear consensus topology for *Sindora*, *Copaifera* and *Daniellia*. This is observed in all datasets regardless of the method of paralog handling, which shows that the signal of ILS and possible hybridisation persists despite our best efforts to exclude or separate paralogs.

### 4.3. Recommendations for paralogy detection



Overall, the following recommendations are given to guide choice of paralog detection in the absence of a reference genome or prior genomic knowledge for the study group, although the choice of method will be influenced by the taxonomic breadth, history of WGD, and paralog characteristics in the study group. Firstly, in deciding between which assembly pipeline to use, CAPTUS performs better at recovering all possible paralogous gene copies compared to HybPiper and reduces the incidence of "artifactual orthologs", where a multi-copy gene only has a single copy recovered because of insufficient sequencing depth, assembly settings and divergent probes, although the last reason is less likely here due to the use of taxon-specific Detarioideae baits. Across all methods of paralog detection, the heterozygosity-based method PPD was the most accurate, albeit just slightly more so than the built-in paralog detection in CAPTUS, although the default parameters need to be optimised for each dataset. A similar heterozygosity-based method, HybPhaser, was also tested with similarly positive results. These methods allow the identification of genes with paralogs and their exclusion from further analyses, which is a commonly used method to ensure the dataset contains single copy genes. In our case study the removal of paralogous genes did not lead to large changes in species tree topology, which suggests that the phylogenetic signal is stronger than the noise from including paralogous sequences.

In all the similarity-based paralog detection methods, there is no completely objective way of differentiating true paralogs from just allelic variation. Additionally, an indication of potential paralogy from any of the similarity-based methods may not necessarily mean that multiple gene copies were successfully recovered. Tree-based methods such as the Yang & Smith pipeline provide an alternative to distinguish alleles from paralogs that can be separated for use in subsequent species tree construction. As our dataset involves a history of whole genome duplication leading to many duplicated genes and extensive paralogy, the most theoretically sound method would be to use an assembly pipeline to recover as many gene copies and as few chimeric sequences as possible and then use the Yang & Smith tree-based approach to sort gene copies into their respective paralogs, which can then be treated separately as individual orthologs when generating species trees. In our case we would recommend the use of CAPTUS over HybPiper because of its higher paralog recovery rates, although this may depend on the study group.

## 5. Conclusion



In this case study using Detarioideae legume clades, the best method for identifying paralogs in the absence of a reference genome was through PPD. However, this method is currently only designed to be used with sequences assembled with HybPiper and requires optimised parameters that relate to the taxonomic breadth of the study group. The next most accurate method is the default paralog identification methods of CAPTUS which recovered more paralogous genes than HybPiper. PPD and the default paralog detection in HybPiper and CAPTUS are useful for identifying genes with paralogs to exclude them from further analyses. However, if the aim is to separate the various paralog copies and produce individual gene trees for each copy recovered, short of having a reference genome for the study group, we would recommend using the Yang & Smith RT approach on CAPTUS-generated data.

Whilst different methods of handling paralogues resulted in datasets of different sizes and non-identical sets of genes from the Detarioideae bait set, the resulting species trees were largely similar topologically. Any differences may reflect areas of the topology affected by real biological issues such as ILS and reticulation. It seems that for the Detarioid legumes, the genes represented in the bait set provide sufficient common phylogenetic signal that emerges across diverse methods of paralogy handling. However, this may differ across bait sets used and taxonomic groups studied and we encourage future empirical studies comparing approaches to paralog detection in target capture DNA sequence data from other plant clades.
