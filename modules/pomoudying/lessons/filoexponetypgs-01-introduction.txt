# Comparison of paralog identification methods and their impact on species tree topologies in target capture phylogenomics within the *Sindora* clade (Detarioideae: Leguminosae)



## Abstract



Target capture is a common method of generating high throughput DNA sequencing data for phylogenetic reconstruction of species relationships, for which single copy genes are usually most informative. However, a pervasive problem with target capture is that putatively single copy genes may in fact be paralogs resulting from gene duplication, which are problematic for phylogenetic inference because their evolutionary history may differ from the divergence history of species. Here, we use as a case study a target enrichment dataset of 88 species of Detarioideae (Leguminosae) with a focus on the *Sindora* clade to examine approaches for handling paralogs, including the built-in paralog handling functions in HybPiper and CAPTUS, plus subsequent steps using Putative Paralog Detection and the tree-based Yang & Smith orthology inference approach. We compare the paralogs flagged using these methods and verify their performance with BLAST mapping against a reference genome sequence of *Sindora glabra*, and then subsequently compare the species tree topologies produced across these methods. Our comparisons of paralogs flagged across the *Sindora* clade show that the Putative Paralog Detection pipeline was the most accurate in identifying paralogs in terms of its similarity to the BLAST mapping, followed by the built-in paralog identification function of CAPTUS. However, the results we recovered for the Detarioideae subfamily suggest that the largest differences in species tree topology resulted from the use of paralog-filtered alignments (such as with the Putative Paralog Detection pipeline and the Yang & Smith orthology inference approaches) rather than just by removing the sequences of identified paralogous genes. This was true for HybPiper-assembled datasets but was not seen in CAPTUS-assembled datasets. In all comparisons, the topological differences caused by different paralog handling methods tended to be confined to clades where processes such as hybridisation and introgression are prevalent. Our study provides a roadmap to establish the best approach to identify, eliminate or separate paralogs in the absence of a chromosomally contiguous reference genome for a study group, and highlights the importance of careful data inspection and processing in addition to understanding the extent of paralogy and paralog characteristics (e.g., sequence divergence between copies) for their study group.

## 1. Introduction



Paralogy is one of the many causes of gene tree conflict, which can interfere with the accurate reconstruction of phylogenetic relationships inferred from DNA sequence data. Paralogs are homologous genes that result from duplication, in contrast to orthologs, which are homologous genes that have arisen by speciation. There are two types of gene duplication which can result in paralog formation: whole genome duplication (WGD), which involves the entire genome, or smaller scale gene duplication events, which include tandem, proximal, dispersed or transposed duplication. Paralogs are common in flowering plants, as WGD events have been recorded in all plant families across the evolutionary history of angiosperms. If cross-species paralogs instead of orthologs are compared, this leads to difficulties in reconstructing phylogenetic relationships, as the evolutionary history of paralogs may not be identical to the evolutionary history of species. The inclusion of paralogous genes and their different evolutionary histories add noise to the phylogenetic signal, which may also affect downstream analyses such as divergence time estimates, biogeographic reconstructions and comparative studies of trait evolution.

### 1.1. Paralogy in target capture data



Many phylogenetic studies use hybrid capture (target capture) as a reduced-representation genome sequencing approach in which selected putatively single copy genes from one or several reference genomes or transcriptomes are used to generate a 'bait set' that is used to 'capture' the genes of interest across the study group. After the genes have been selected and sequenced, pipelines such as HybPiper or CAPTUS are used to assemble the target sequences.

However, it is difficult to ensure that all the genes are single copy in all the taxa sequenced, because baits are selected based on inferred single copy status in the reference taxa used, but the gene status of other taxa may differ because of other gene or genome duplication events. For this reason, HybPiper and CAPTUS assembly pipelines have subsequent steps to identify and eliminate putative paralogs. There are several other subsequent pipelines for handling paralogs, including Putative Paralog Detection, which is designed to work with HybPiper output. Another is the Target Enrichment Orthology pipeline (hereafter referred to as the Yang & Smith pipeline), which aims to separate paralogs using a phylogenetic tree-based analysis.

### 1.2. Approaches to paralog handling



The various approaches to handling paralogs can be classified into two main types, which are similarity-based and tree-based methods.

Similarity-based methods pick out potential paralogs based on their sequence identity to the known copy of the gene studied, and these include methods such as the built-in paralog flagging methods of both HybPiper and CAPTUS, along with the subsequent Putative Paralog Detection (PPD) pipeline for HybPiper assemblies. In HybPiper, reference-guided read extraction with BLAST followed by de novo assembly with SPAdes produces the assembled sequences for each gene. Subsequently, the `paralog_retriever` command works by identifying genes for which multiple sequences have been recovered if these are sufficiently long (at least 75% of the reference length). The longest copy is assigned as the main copy, or if the sequence lengths are the same, the sequence with the highest similarity to the reference is assigned to be the main one, while all others are listed as paralogs. In the fully de novo assembly-based pipeline CAPTUS, after sequence assembly with MEGAHIT, sequences are assigned to their respective genes based on their similarity to the reference sequences using BLAT. If multiple sequences are assigned to the same gene, they are ordered based on their similarity and coverage with respect to the reference sequence, and by default the top five paralogs are kept and aligned with the best match (i.e., the main copy), although typically only the main copy will be used for generating phylogenetic trees.

The Putative Paralog Detection (PPD) pipeline works by mapping reads to the HybPiper assembled sequences to generate sequences showing ambiguities (or heterozygosity). If enough taxa share the same heterozygous sites, the gene will be flagged as potentially paralogous because such variability is unlikely to reflect allelic differences, and additional trimming can be carried out to remove regions which have incorporated paralogous reads from the alignments.

In all these similarity-based methods based on HybPiper, CAPTUS and PPD, there are drawbacks. All assume that genes that are not flagged as paralogues are single copy and orthologous, which might not be the case because of hidden orthology, where only one copy of a multicopy gene is present. Hidden orthology can happen because of data processing artifacts such as insufficient sequencing depth or the failure of target capture to recover all copies, or via biological processes such as pseudo-orthology where differential loss after gene duplication leads to the presence of a single but non-orthologous gene copy across species. In addition, all these methods may flag genes as paralogs when in fact the variation is allelic.

Tree-based methods such as the Yang & Smith pipeline use a phylogenetic approach to infer paralogs and orthologs. The Yang & Smith pipeline implements both the "monophyletic outgroups" (MO) and "rooted outgroups" (RT) methods. The MO approach generates a tree with duplicated taxa (i.e., paralogs) removed, while the RT approach retains all separate paralog trees, which allows the evolutionary history of the duplicated gene copies to also be recovered. Tree-based methods allow for allelic variation and true paralogy to be differentiated in duplicated gene copies by removing lineage-specific duplicates in a preprocessing step, but they are dependent on the sequence assembly pipelines such as HybPiper and CAPTUS because these produce the input sequences for the phylogenetic analyses.

Attempts have been made to compare methods of paralog identification and understand the effect of paralogs on phylogenetic inference. While some have found that paralog inclusion has minimal impacts on phylogenetic reconstruction, others have found that paralogs can have a negative impact on species tree topologies and support values. We build on our current understanding of how paralogs affect phylogenetic inference by evaluating the performance of similarity-based methods of paralog detection—including the `paralog_retriever` function of HybPiper, the built-in paralog identification pipeline in CAPTUS and the Putative Paralog Detection pipeline which works on HybPiper-assembled sequences—in comparison to an approach that uses a chromosomal reference genome to identify paralogs with confidence. We then analyse the impact of paralog handling with similarity- and tree-based approaches (MO and RT methods of Yang & Smith) on both coalescent-based and concatenated species trees.

We test these paralog identification pipelines using the *Sindora* clade from subfamily Detarioideae of the legume family (Fabaceae, Leguminosae). The Detarioideae is one of the early-diverging legume subfamilies, with 81 genera and c. 760 species. The *Sindora* clade is part of the Detarieae tribe, which consists of 21 genera, with 11 restricted to Africa, two endemic to Madagascar, two restricted to Asia, and two found only in the Neotropics. The *Sindora* clade consists of five closely related genera: *Sindora*, *Copaifera*, *Tessmannia*, *Sindoropsis*, and *Detarium*. *Sindora* is a genus of 20 species, with 19 species in tropical and subtropical Asia, and one species in West Tropical Africa. *Copaifera* is a pantropical genus of 39 species, with 34 species in South America, four species in Africa and one species in Borneo in Southeast Asia. *Tessmannia*, *Sindoropsis* and *Detarium* are found only in Africa, and they have 24 species, one species and three species respectively. The most recent phylogenetic study of the group has found that the intergeneric relationships among the *Sindora* clade are not well resolved.

We investigate the performance of paralog identification methods through a target capture-based study of the *Sindora* clade using a bait set for the Detarioideae, which was designed based on transcriptome sequences from four Detarioid legumes: *Anthonotha fragrans*, *Afzelia bella*, *Copaifera officinalis* and *Prioria balsamifera*. For each of the four transcriptomes, protein sequence orthology was established by clustering, followed by removing protein sequences present in multiple copies. The remaining protein sequences were further checked for orthology using a phylogenetic approach. As the orthology status of these genes chosen as baits may differ in species other than the four reference transcriptomes used, we verify the paralog status of genes across samples from the *Sindora* clade using the available reference genome of *Sindora glabra*. The *Sindora* clade is an ideal candidate for such a study because the Leguminosae family has experienced several WGD events in its history and there is also one more recent WGD event common to all members of subfamily Detarioideae. Although the bait set was designed for Detarioideae, we expect that across the group diploidisation or localised duplications will have resulted in different copy number variation for many of the target loci across the entire set of species of the *Sindora* clade.

In addition to comparing the paralogs flagged, we also compare the species tree topologies produced by the various paralog handling methods. Our aim is to provide a much-needed comparative assessment of methods for paralog handling in target capture datasets and their impact on the subsequent tree topology, to facilitate the selection of an appropriate pipeline for studies where prior genomic knowledge is scarce.
